Binomial
Distribution

Binomial Distribution is one of the most important probability distributions in HSC Mathematics. These notes are designed for quick revision, maximum step-marks, and MCQ tricks.

Core Concepts & Conditions

A distribution is called a Binomial Distribution only if it arises from Bernoulli Trials.

  • Fixed Trials (n): Number of trials must be finite and fixed.
  • Dichotomous Outcomes: Each trial has exactly two outcomes — Success or Failure.
  • Constant Probability (p): Probability remains same for every trial.
  • Independence: All trials must be independent.

HSC Exam Tip: Sampling with replacement ensures independence (Binomial). Sampling without replacement is not Binomial because probabilities change.

Notation & PMF

Define the random variable formally before solving.

Let X ~ B(n,p)
P(X=x) = ⁿCₓ · pˣ · q⁽ⁿ⁻ˣ⁾
  • n = Total number of trials
  • x = Required number of successes
  • p = Probability of success
  • q = Probability of failure = (1-p)
  • nCx = Combination formula
ⁿCₓ = n! / [x!(n−x)!]

Parameters & Formulas

Quantity Formula Meaning
Mean E(X) = np Long-term average outcome
Variance V(X) = npq Spread of the distribution
Standard Deviation σ = √npq Square root of variance

High-Yield MCQ Property: Mean is always greater than Variance because q is always less than 1.

Problem Solving Template

  1. Define the random variable X.
  2. Declare the distribution: X ~ B(n,p).
  3. Identify n, p and q.
  4. Write the general PMF first.
  5. Substitute values and simplify.

Calculation Tip: HSC board accepts exponent form. Do not waste time fully expanding huge calculations.

Quick Shortcuts & Time-Saving Exam Tricks

The “At Least 1” Shortcut

This is one of the most repeated HSC question formats. If the question asks for the probability of getting at least one success, do not waste time calculating:

P(X=1) + P(X=2) + P(X=3) + ...

Instead, use the complement rule because calculating the probability of getting zero successes is much faster.

P(X ≥ 1) = 1 - P(X = 0)

Exam Trick: Always look for opportunities to use complements. It saves time and reduces calculation mistakes.

Handling “At Least / At Most” Boundaries

Carefully read the wording in probability questions. Terms like at least and at most are extremely common in exams.

“At least 2 successes” means: P(X ≥ 2) = 1 - [P(X=0) + P(X=1)]
“At most 4 successes” means: P(X ≤ 4) = 1 - P(X=5)

Important: “At least” usually starts from the given number and goes upward. “At most” includes all values up to the given number.

Symmetric Distributions

When the probability of success becomes exactly:

p = 0.5

the Binomial Distribution becomes perfectly symmetrical. This usually happens in unbiased coin toss problems.

P(X = x) = P(X = n - x)

Example: If n = 5, then:

P(X=1) = P(X=4)

Time Saver: If you already calculated one side of a symmetric distribution, you instantly know the opposite side without extra work.

Hidden Variable Decoding

Sometimes the value of p is hidden inside English sentences. You must first convert the statement into algebra.

Example statement: “An event succeeds twice as often as it fails.”

p = 2q

Since probabilities always satisfy:

p + q = 1

substitute p = 2q:

2q + q = 1
3q = 1
q = 1/3
p = 2/3

High-Scoring Habit: Whenever probability information is hidden in words, immediately convert it into equations before solving.

Last Minute Revision Checklist

  • Always verify p + q = 1
  • x starts from 0 and ends at n
  • ΣP(X=x) = 1
  • Check whether question asks Mean (np) or Variance (npq)
  • Use complement rule whenever possible
